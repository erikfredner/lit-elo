What do language models consider canonical?

Introduction

A paradox: The literary canon is comprised of a finite number of works and authors, yet it cannot be comprehensively enumerated.

Since canonicity is always contested, it is better understood as a continuum than a binary. Some authors and works are more canonical than others. There is a great difference between authors and works that have attained some degree of canonicity and those that have attained none. But there is also a great difference between those that are highly canonical and those whose status is more uncertain.

Scholars may object to this framing on principle, but it is difficult to refute in practice. For example, who would sincerely argue that Bret Harte is as canonical as Toni Morrison, or that “The Luck of Roaring Camp” as canonical as Beloved?

Such comparisons are rarely or never made explicitly. The consensus that Harte is “obviously” less canonical than Morrison goes unrecorded. Making implicit views explicit is valuable because then they can be studied and contested. While it would be ideal to ask literary scholars to make hundreds of thousands of such comparisons, it is also prohibitively difficult. As a first step, we can try to simulate them.

Why?

All proxies for the literary canon have weaknesses. Anthologies structurally disfavor prose, especially the novel. Popular culture that engages with literature, such as Jeopardy, provides some insight into the canon, but also falls victim to presentism.

Language models (LMs) have been trained on data legally and illegally scraped from the internet. This provides another such proxy measure for these judgments. While LMs like ChatGPT and Claude are increasingly being used to simulate aspects of culture, they have not yet been used to simulate judgments of canonicity to my knowledge.

“You are a professor”

I provide each LM with the following system prompt:

You are a professor of American literatures. Which is more canonical?

Answer 1 or 2.

The model is then presented with a choice, formatted like so:

1: Bret Harte

2: Toni Morrison

Or, in the case of works, like so:

1: The Luck of Roaring Camp — Bret Harte

2: Beloved — Toni Morrison

The LM returns the integer indicating its choice. Each pairing is put to the model twice, once in each order, because LMs tend to favor whichever option is listed first. If the model picks the same item both times, that item wins. If its answer changes with the order, the pairing counts as a draw.

This process can be repeated many times using many different comparisons for a trivial cost. Through OpenRouter’s batch API, about 270,000 such judgments from four models cost about $10.50. They could run for even less by using local LMs.

Why “professor of American literatures?”

The hard part of this task is identifying which authors and works to compare, and what the principle of comparison ought to be. It is well established in the research that the school is the place where the literary canon is institutionalized, nowhere more so than the university. This role for the LM specifies the type of canonicity in question.

Through previous research, I have access to data describing records in The MLA International Bibliography (MLAIB) pertaining to literatures in English. The authors and works represented in this project include those designated as part of American literature by the bibliographers. Works and authors are also filtered to roughly the top 5% by number of MLAIB records to reduce the number of total comparisons required. For authors, that means 38 or more records; for works, 13 or more. About 800 authors and 1,500 works remain.

One could object that limiting to roughly the top 5% of authors is already quite exclusionary. Yet the threshold still admits writers whom few general readers would recognize, such as David Bottoms and John Ehle, alongside writers as widely read as Ta-Nehisi Coates and Sharon Olds, all of whom have 38 or 39 records.

Four judges

Any single LM’s judgments reflect its own training data and quirks. So rather than rely on one model, I ask four models from four makers to judge the same pairings independently: Anthropic’s claude-haiku-5.5, OpenAI’s gpt-6-luna, DeepSeek’s deepseek-v4.1-flash, and TypeSafe’s jev-1.14. The first three are small, inexpensive chat models. Jev is a different kind of model: it does not write text at all, but answers typed questions about a situation it is given. It receives the same prompt as its instructions and chooses between the same two options.

Because every judge sees the same pairings, their verdicts can be compared directly. The main leaderboards pool all four judges’ comparisons. Each model also has its own leaderboard, reached from the links above each leaderboard, and each author and work page shows where every model ranks it. The Judges page reports how decisive each model is, how often its choice survives swapping the order, how strongly it favors whichever option is listed first, and how often it agrees with the others.

Evaluation

Because of the nature of the canon described above, it is not possible to say with precision whether the models’ judgments are “correct.” But there are several measures that can be used to see how they differ from other proxies for the canon, and from one another.

MLAIB

The MLAIB itself implicitly ranks authors and works by indicating the degree of attention they have received from indexed literary scholarship and by the bibliographers who create these records. Big changes in rank between the Elo ranking implied in the relative position of an author or work in the absolute number of articles assigned to them and their status can be explained by two factors. One is time: The more contemporary the writer or work, the less time they have had to be studied, and the fewer times they could have possibly been the subject of an indexed piece.

At the level of the individual author and work, this is recorded in the line charts that show the history of the entity’s Elo ranking. The left side represents their implicit Elo ranking based on MLAIB records, whereas the right side shows their relative position after simulated comparisons by all four judges.

The leaderboards and each author and work page also show this starting point as a rank: the MLAIB rank orders items by their record counts, and Change is the number of places an item has gained (▲) or lost (▼) since then. Items with the same number of records share a starting rank, so many lesser-studied works start tied.

Ratings and uncertainty

The leaderboards are ordered by a single statistical fit (a Bradley–Terry model) over every comparison the judges have made, not by the running Elo score. Each model’s leaderboard uses a separate fit over that model’s comparisons alone. The fit asks which set of ratings best explains all of the outcomes at once, so it does not depend on the order in which comparisons happened. Ratings use the same scale as Elo: a 400-point gap means the higher item is expected to win about ten times out of eleven. Draws count as half a win for each item.

Each rating comes with a 95% interval (±), drawn as a grey bar on the leaderboard, and each item has a likely rank: the range of leaderboard positions it occupies in 95% of plausible versions of the ratings. Neighbours whose ranges overlap cannot be told apart with the comparisons gathered so far. To narrow these ranges where it matters most, the highest-rated authors and works are also compared directly against one another in extra rounds.

The Elo score is still shown on each author and work page, and its history is what the line charts trace. The fitted ratings guide which items are compared next: each pairing matches one of the least-compared items with an opponent of similar rating, where the outcome is least certain.

Convergence

Due to the autoregressive properties of LMs, every generated response has a chance of containing a hallucination. In this case, that would require the model to choose the “wrong” integer in its output (e.g., selecting Harte over Morrison). Asking each question in both orders, and of four models rather than one, helps distinguish such noise from the models’ considered views.

Over a large number of iterations, Elo scores change less because authors and works have been compared to one another and to similarly ranked entities previously. When this happens, outcomes become less informative, except in cases where an expected outcome is reversed (e.g., selecting “Roaring Camp” over Beloved). As the number of matches increases, changes in Elo rankings decrease.
